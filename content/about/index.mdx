# About

<Mark type="annotate" gesture="hook" color="blue"></Mark><Mark color="blue">“Art is Easy.”</Mark> Few phrases are as closely tied to composer and conceptual artist <Mark color="blue">Giuseppe Chiari</Mark> (Florence, 1926–2007). Within the Northwestern Archives we find this statement repeated across scores, exhibitions, performances, even emblazoned on postcards and paper bags. This exhibition proposes to read “Art is Easy” as <Mark color="yellow">a mantra</Mark>: a refrain whose meaning deepens with each repetition.

<Mark color="yellow">‘Art is Easy’</Mark> presumes a complex definition of art as both <Mark color="yellow">immediately accessible and deeply meaningful</Mark>, even philosophical. Working both within and against an artistic tradition that values precision and virtuosity, Chiari explored the artistic possibilities of everyday objects and gestures. For Chiari, art might be ‘easy’ to produce, but its meaning is <Mark color="red">anything but simple</Mark>. In so many of his works <Mark color="blue">the boundaries between art and life, performer and audience</Mark>, remain open to question.

In <Mark color="red">“Art is Easy”</Mark>, meaning is found in process and creation: <Mark color="yellow">anyone can play and any gesture or sound can become music</Mark>. This is where Chiari’s statement reaches its broadest stakes. His <Mark color="red">radically democratic proposition</Mark> asks us to approach our daily activities as acts of artistic creation. This online exhibition is an extension of his philosophy. We invite you to <Mark color="green">act on, in, and with Chiari’s work</Mark> through a variety of immersive modes. We encourage you to explore all of the facets of “Art is Easy” as you watch, read, listen, and play.

## Giuseppe Chiari

Giuseppe Chiari was born on September 26, 1926 in Florence, the city where he lived for the entirety of his life. He began studying engineering,which he later abandoned to study piano and composition privately. He would eventually devote himself entirely to music, progressively steering his exploration towards experimental practices. In 1960, together with Pietro Grossi, he founded the association Vita Musicale Contemporanea, bringing together musicians and artists dedicated to experimenting with new sonic languages.

Against this backdrop, he embarked on a path of international significance, connecting with Sylvano Bussotti and collaborating with him on the realization of the traveling exhibition Musica e segno, which was presented in Rome at the Galleria Numero and subsequently at the Creative and Performing Arts Center in Buffalo, New York.  In 1962, he joined the international Fluxus movement at the Festspiele Neuester Musik festival in Wiesbaden, where Frederic Rzewski performed the work Gesti sul piano. A piece which foregrounds gesture as the constitutive part of the score, an unfolding process documented through sound and image. In 1963, in Florence, he participated in the activities of Gruppo 70, contributing to research on the visual value of text. That same year, Chiari’s Teatrino was performed in New York as part of a concert series organized by Charlotte Moorman and Nam June Paik. Throughout the 1960s Chiari’s artistic practice increasingly concerned the relationship between art and  everyday life. He created works like Suonare la citta, where the sonic capacities of quotidian objects and activities are explored, the entire urban environment conceived as a potential musical instrument. From the 1970s onwards, Chiari  developed a body of work focused on the use of mixed-media techniques, creating collages on paper, sheet music, musical instruments, and wooden supports that were progressively overlaid with chromatic interventions. In this context, objects are stripped of their original function and redefined as visual surfaces, while retaining a conceptual significance linked to the musical dimension, consistent with his exploration of the transformation of sound into gesture, and gesture into sign. He died in Florence on May 9, 2007.

## Postword

Chiari never worked within a single discipline. A score could become a drawing, a sentence could become a score, and a piano could be something to touch, strike or photograph rather than simply play. Music, word, image and gesture continually pass into and through one another, until the boundaries between them disappear.

This is what Fluxus artist Dick Higgins called intermedia: an art form which falls “between media”, creating an open space between artistic languages and everyday life. Chiari made that space his own. His works do not ask to be contemplated from a distance. Rather, they invite us to experience, repeat and perform them. Seen this way, “Art is Easy” does not mean art is simple. Instead, it can happen anywhere, to anyone at any time.
